# Writing voice for this site

This is a café's website, not a lifestyle brand. The copy should read like
the owners describing their own place plainly — not like marketing copy,
not like a magazine, and not like AI-generated text. This section exists
because the same handful of AI-sounding patterns kept getting rewritten out
and then slipping back in across many editing passes. Check new or edited
copy against this list before treating it as done.

## Hard rule: no antithesis/reveal constructions

**Never write "not X, it's Y," "X, not Y," "the one that isn't," "not just
X," or any other setup-then-contrastive-turn sentence.** This is the
single most consistent tell and it keeps recurring even after dedicated
cleanup passes — it hides in small phrases, not just big rhetorical
paragraphs. Examples actually caught and removed from this codebase:

- "Choosing it was deliberate, not aspirational."
- "None of this is about having the fanciest machine on the street. It is
  about the cup being right every time."
- "We wanted to be the one place on this road that isn't [built around a
  single occasion]."
- "...a purpose across the whole day, not just one part of it."
- "Most cafés size their espresso setup around the morning rush. We sized
  ours for the evening instead." / "induction, not gas." / "...instead of
  speed."

**This is a structural problem, not just a wording problem.** A whole
paragraph built as "here's what other places do (list), here's what we do
differently (summary)" is the same antithesis move stretched across
several sentences — rewording the ending doesn't fix it even if no single
sentence literally says "not X." A journal post about why Artha runs four
service windows does not need a systematic survey of five named nearby
businesses and what each one lacks; that is competitive-positioning copy,
not something an owner casually mentions. When explaining a choice, state
what Artha does and why, and cut the "most cafés do X" setup rather than
trying to reword it — the comparison itself is the tell, not just "not"
or "instead."

If a sentence pits two things against each other to land a point, rewrite
it as a plain positive statement instead.

## Other patterns to avoid

- **Em-dash overuse.** One or two per paragraph at most, not one per
  sentence.
- **Rule-of-three / tricolon lists** used as a rhetorical device (list
  three parallel things, then land a thesis sentence). Fine for genuinely
  listing facts (menu items, hours); not fine as a persuasion structure.
- **Essay architecture**: setup → list of supporting facts → a
  thesis-restating closer, repeated paragraph after paragraph. Real
  owner-written copy is flatter and less argued.
- **Magazine-style H2 subheads** that are themselves punchy mini-theses
  rather than plain section labels.
- **Lyrical/lifestyle-brand phrasing** reaching for a mood instead of
  stating the fact: "the first pour of the morning," "built for slowing
  down," "linger," "a space where X meets Y." Say the plain thing (actual
  hours, actual offering) instead.
- **Repeated stock phrases across different pages** (e.g. "built for
  ___" showed up six times verbatim across six different sections before
  being caught) — a dead giveaway of generated text, even when each
  individual instance reads fine in isolation.
- **Passive/third-person "report" voice** ("is treated," "was chosen,"
  "the café runs on that pace") instead of the owners actually talking
  about their own place in first person ("we," "our"). Use active voice
  and natural contractions — real owner-written copy sounds like someone
  talking, not a Wikipedia entry.

## What's fine

- Short, punchy headline-style taglines ("A full vegetarian table, taken
  seriously.") — these function as titles, not prose, and get more
  latitude than body copy.
- Plain, impersonal menu/hours/disclaimer text — that's the actual
  convention for that kind of content, confirmed against real competitor
  sites (Karafa, Coffee Katha, Theory Cafe, Paakashala, La Vie En Rose).
- Explaining what "Artha" means in a plain, encyclopedic register (the
  Sanskrit root, the four Purusharthas) rather than a crafted soundbite —
  this is informational content, treat it like a reference entry.

## Before calling a copy pass done

Re-read every edited sentence once specifically hunting for the
antithesis pattern above. It has survived at least three dedicated
cleanup sweeps in this project by hiding in increasingly small phrases —
assume it's still there until you've checked line by line, not just
skimmed for tone.
